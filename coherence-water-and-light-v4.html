Teacher notes · model, limits and textbook connection

Teaching sequence. Chapter 5 pp. 63, 69 and 72: common drive, changing phase difference and coherent antiphase sources. Chapter 6 pp. 108–109: one source and two slits versus two independent LEDs. Chapter 6 pp. 147–148: HKDSE 2015 Paper 1B Q7 and the accompanying report. “Out of phase” is not a synonym for “incoherent”.

Water model. Equal component amplitudes A = 1, speed v = 1 model distance unit per model time unit and carrier frequency f = 0.8 cycles per model time unit. λ = v/f = 1.25. Sources are separated by 3 distance units. At P, each contribution is cos[2πf(t − r/v) + ψⱼ(t − r/v)]. Phase changes propagate outwards; already emitted waves are not reset. Fixed-phase cases produce exact permanent nodes. Independently wandering drivers have smooth timing fluctuations, so the displayed envelope is not a constant amplitude and their cancellation guides are not permanent nodes. Equal amplitude everywhere, no attenuation, boundary reflections or startup transients are assumed. P remains fixed during playback.

Version 2: finite-packet lesson. Stage 2 is a simplified classical atomic-source illustration, not a literal picture of an LED or of individual photon shapes. Each independent emission has a finite smooth envelope and an unrelated initial carrier phase. Its electric field is A(t − x/v − t₀) cos[2πf(t − x/v − t₀) + φ₀]. The envelope vanishes outside the packet; emitted packets continue propagating unchanged when a later event occurs. Both sources have the same central frequency. Their emission times, phases and emitting atoms are independently generated. Few-emission mode deliberately separates contributions to make them visible. Many-emission mode adds the overlapping fields rather than drawing a single endless packet.

Packet comparison. The vertical marker samples both lanes at the same propagation distance. Each pointer uses the phase of the field actually present there, including travel time. Between packets the field is zero, and no phase is displayed. Very weak fields are also hidden (amplitude below 0.035 model units). In many-emission mode the pointers show the phase of each source’s combined complex field. Within a pair of equal-frequency packets the phase difference stays fixed; it is not maintained across independent pairs. This distinction is the learning target. Gaps are not the cause of incoherence.

Teaching controls. Next emission pauses just after an emission begins. Next overlapping pair pauses inside the next overlap, so both phases can be compared. Automatic overlap pausing applies to few-emission mode and pauses once for each new pair. Many-emission mode restarts time and disables these pair-specific controls. Packet playback defaults to 1×; the other stages retain their own speed setting. At 1×, Stage 2 advances two model-time units per real second. All displayed times and distances are schematic.

Version 4: the same separated packets in Stages 2 and 3. Stage 3 reuses Stage 2's few-emission schedule, smooth finite envelopes, carrier phases, propagation delays and drawing function. Successive packets from each source are separated by empty gaps; no continuous combined trace is drawn. It opens at the first overlapping pair from Stage 2. The next-pattern button advances to the next overlapping pair. Phase pointers disappear when the corresponding packet is absent or below the same 0.035 amplitude threshold used in Stage 2. Independent pairs do not maintain their relative phase; a shared source supplies identical packet copies, including the gaps. Gaps themselves do not cause incoherence. Only a few emissions are shown for clarity; ordinary sources contain many overlapping contributions.

Stage 3 screen and eye average. The screen isolates phase stability. Each visible arriving packet is assigned component intensity I₀ = 1; an absent or very weak packet contributes zero. When both arrive, I(x,t) = 2 + 2 cos[2πx/Δx + δ(t)], using the same retarded packet phases as the dial. With only one packet it is uniform; between packets it is dark. The eye view shows the ideal long-observation intensity average, including the gaps. The mean visible fraction is D = (7.5/12.5)[1 − (2/π) asin(√0.035)], from the mean packet duration, mean separation and envelope threshold. Independent sources give Ī(x) = 2D; identical shared packets give Ī(x) = D[2 + 2 cos(2πx/Δx)]. Thus gaps reduce mean brightness but do not wash out coherent fringes. This is an ensemble-average limit, not a short browser-rate accumulation. Equal mean illumination and a broad constant diffraction envelope are assumed. The screen averages intensity over time at each position, not signed fields or neighbouring pixels; the packet envelopes are retained in the source drawing but amplitude variations within each visible packet are normalised on the screen.

Stage 3 teaching controls. Slow motion and What the eye sees use the same screen location. Switching view preserves the emission time and playback state. Next pattern advances to a later, distinct independent-source phase relationship and pauses; for coherent light it advances time while preserving the fringe positions. It is disabled in the eye view. Both source and phase animations remain slowed for explanation in either mode. Playback speed changes only the animation speed, not the calculated eye average. Independent and coherent source selections preserve the viewing mode but restart the displayed stream. The numerical averaging-window, position and visibility controls are absent from Stage 3; the Stage 4 exploration retains its controls.

Textbook connection. The generic atomic illustration explains independent emission at HKDSE level. The textbook’s two LEDs are another example of independent sources, although an LED emits through electron–hole recombination in a semiconductor. Neither illustration is a literal animation of individual photon shapes. Real sources can also vary in intensity, spectrum, polarisation and emission direction.

Ordinary light. Many independent emission processes contribute to the light. The atomic packets and LED emitting centres are simplified teaching representations, not a literal count or a one-to-one photon animation. Wave traces represent electric field, not a sideways path travelled by light. Narrow-band light from both LEDs has the same central wavelength, 650 nm. A finite wave train has a spread of frequencies: this is not a model of two infinitely long, perfectly monochromatic sine waves whose phase changes arbitrarily.

Two time scales. An actual optical cycle is far shorter than a phase-renewal interval, which in turn is far shorter than the eye’s observation time. The drawing greatly enlarges these scales separately and compresses the number of oscillations in a train. Model cycles, phase intervals and playback speed are teaching units, not measured LED times. In Stage 3 the packet transit to the comparison marker is included; the remaining common flight time to the screen is omitted. In Stage 4 the source sketch, phase dial and screen follow a related emission interval with the common flight time omitted. Relative path phase is included. This isolates phase stability; it is not a full simulation of the spatial extent, polarisation and spectrum of an LED.

Stage 4 light calculation. We use equal parallel-polarised scalar contributions, each of intensity I₀ = 1. For an interval with relative source phase δ = ψ₁ − ψ₂ and screen position x, I(x,t) = 2 + 2 cos[2πx/Δx + δ(t)]. This short-time intensity has already been averaged over optical cycles. The lower strip averages I over elapsed phase intervals, using the selected trailing window. It never averages signed electric fields to determine brightness. Equivalently, Ī = 2 + 2 Re{exp(i2πx/Δx) ⟨exp(iδ)⟩}. Independent phase renewals make the average complex factor approach zero; a finite sample may leave faint residual fringes. Accumulation is a numerical time average, not an image-blur effect.

Geometry and brightness. The comparison fixes λ = 650 nm, separation a = 0.325 mm and D = 3.00 m, giving fringe separation Δx = λD/a = 6.00 mm in the small-angle approximation. The two-LED schematic uses the same effective separation to isolate coherence, rather than reproducing the 6 mm separation in the examination question. Equal illumination and a broad, constant diffraction envelope are assumed over the displayed screen. Screen colour is a display mapping of intensity, not a calibrated brightness measurement. A smooth illuminated background is not zero light.

Shared source. A laser illuminates both narrow slits coherently. The optional filtered, small-source example gives both slits the same changing phase history; the pattern stays steady because their relative phase stays fixed. That is an approximation for suitable spatial filtering and path delays small compared with the wave-train duration. It does not imply that any extended lamp, any pair of slits or arbitrarily large path differences will preserve coherence. The imposed common phase shift is an explanatory comparison, not a control normally found on a laser pointer.

Phase convention. Phase increases anticlockwise from the top, with the cosine reference: 0° is maximum positive displacement or positive field. The directed arc runs from S₂ to S₁. Light-pointer rotation is deliberately slowed; the visible rotation rate is not the light frequency. When the pointers overlap, the blue triangular and orange circular tips identify both sources.

Controls. Changing stages or arrangements pauses and restarts the model. Restart preserves display choices. Next phase renewal advances to just after the next source renewal and pauses. Accumulate advances through the selected number of phase intervals and pauses. Display toggles, observation-point changes and averaging-window changes preserve time. The fixed random sequence repeats on Restart, allowing comparisons. There are no external scripts, fonts, images or network requests; this file works offline. All earlier versions remain separate unchanged files.
